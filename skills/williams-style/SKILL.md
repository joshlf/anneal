# Williams-style revision

Make the prose easier for its intended reader to understand and use without
changing what the writer can honestly claim. Serve the requested communicative
purpose, not brevity, simplicity, or visible editing activity.

These are diagnostic principles, not a vocabulary blacklist or fixed prose
template. This is a Williams-inspired workflow, not an imitation or authoritative
digest of Joseph Williams. The document-level guidance is agent-specific.

## Establish the task and preservation boundary

Use the supplied or reasonably inferable audience, purpose, genre, editing scope,
and relevant reader knowledge. Treat reader knowledge as local: general expertise
does not imply familiarity with every codebase, protocol, institution, or
argument.

Identify what the text should accomplish for this reader. Understanding, deciding,
acting, and verifying are common purposes, but supported rhetorical or
interpersonal work may matter too.

Do not turn ordinary context into a questionnaire. When a material premise is
uncertain, preserve it, use a supported least-committal treatment, ask a focused
question when appropriate, or offer labeled alternatives when permitted.

Distinguish drafting from faithful revision, copyediting, summarization,
substantive restructuring, and critique. Do not use a request for local editing
as permission for broader rewriting or semantic selection.

Before drafting, identify the supported answer and relationships and arrange the
prerequisites in the order the reader needs them. Before revising, identify what
the source requires you to preserve. Do not reproduce the writer's or model's
discovery process merely because it is available.

Preserve who did what to whom; what is known and unknown; attribution; causality;
conditions and exceptions; negation; quantities, thresholds, units, comparisons,
timing, ordering, alternatives, and degrees of certainty. Preserve exact technical
identifiers, code, quotations, links, citations, and established terminology
unless changing them is explicitly in scope. Keep citations attached to the claims
they support.

Never make prose clearer by changing its meaning. Do not strengthen or weaken a
claim merely to make it cleaner. In particular:

- Do not turn association into causation, possibility into fact, permission into
  a guarantee, or evidence into proof.
- Do not turn a necessary condition into a sufficient one, or a one-way condition
  such as "only if" or "only when" into an equivalence.
- Do not infer an order between two events merely because both are ordered
  relative to a third.
- Do not resolve an ambiguous actor, pronoun, ownership relation, motive, cause,
  or missing premise by guessing.
- Do not erase an unresolved relationship or narrow its possible referents beyond
  what the source establishes.
- A labeled alternative may assume only the interpretation named in its label;
  related facts do not follow automatically.

When the requested output must contain only revised prose, preserve unresolved
meaning rather than silently choosing an interpretation.

Do not invent an actor, premise, risk, severity, motive, or stake merely to improve
the prose.

## Allocate attention according to the task

Decide what must remain and what deserves prominence before polishing sentences.

First determine whether the task permits semantic selection. A summary or new
draft may permit choosing among supported facts; a faithful revision, copyedit,
protocol, checklist, reference document, audit record, or explicit completeness
request may require preserving all relevant substance. The request governs. Genre
alone does not authorize omission.

When selection is authorized, omit a supported detail only when it does not
materially serve the requested communicative purpose. A detail may matter because
it changes the answer, supplies a prerequisite, preserves uncertainty, affects
action, supports verification, fulfills a completeness commitment, or performs a
supported rhetorical or interpersonal function.

A detail does not deserve primary attention merely because it is true or related.
Rarity or small size does not make an important condition or warning peripheral.

When content must remain, reduce reader effort without deleting it. Compress only
when claims, qualifications, distinctions, and logical relationships survive.
When scope permits changing organization or prominence, move secondary material
only if it remains sufficiently discoverable. Give a condition, caveat,
prerequisite, or uncertainty enough prominence when missing it would materially
undermine the requested communicative purpose.

Prominence can itself be part of meaning, rhetoric, or voice. Preserve deliberate
emphasis unless the task permits changing it.

## Make supported relationships easy to recover

When the material has an important supported relationship—a rule, dependency,
sequence, contrast, grouping, or distinction—make it intelligible when the task
permits changing its presentation. Do not make the reader reconstruct a
load-bearing relation from an undifferentiated catalog.

Do not manufacture hierarchy for tidiness. Preserve independent checklist items,
peer alternatives, separate necessary conditions, and coequal evidence as peers
when that is what the material supports.

When several details support a governing idea, make the relationship visible. If
selection is authorized, retain the support that materially serves the requested
purpose. Otherwise preserve the required substance and improve its organization.

When the source or task establishes a general rule that an example stands in for,
state enough of the rule or its limits for the reader to apply it correctly. Do
not infer a general rule from a case study, quotation, incident, observation, or
narrative merely to make the material look organized.

When drafting or when substantive selection is in scope, keep additional examples,
qualifications, evidence, or restatements while they materially serve the requested
purpose. Corroboration, distinct boundary cases, emphasis, and rhetorical function
can matter even without adding a new concept.

## Make local abstractions earn their cost

Preserve established precise terminology. A technical term is not a defect merely
because it is specialized or unfamiliar. When the reader needs an explanation and
scope permits adding one, explain the term rather than replacing it with a less
precise synonym.

Treat locally coined or ad hoc labels differently, including those already in
editable source prose. Keep one when it adds needed precision, provides a stable
handle for recurring reasoning or retrieval, compresses a repeated relationship,
or usefully unifies material.

If a local label merely renames a one-off mechanism or relationship, state the
mechanism directly when scope permits. When a local abstraction is useful, connect
it clearly enough to what it denotes that the reader need not learn the name and
then reconstruct the underlying relationship.

There is no fixed concept budget, reuse count, or ban on abstraction,
nominalization, or jargon. Keep a nominalization when it names an established
concept, provides useful continuity, or carries precise domain meaning. Rewrite it
when it obscures an important action or participant and an equivalent clearer form
exists.

## Apply Williams-style diagnostics

Once the document-level choices are sound, revise from larger structure toward
smaller expression. Apply only diagnostics that help this passage.

**Shape and coherence.** Give paragraphs identifiable work. Introduce concepts
before relying on them. Begin from information the reader can connect to, then move
toward what is new. Across sentences, preserve topic continuity with repetition,
an unambiguous pronoun, or an explicit bridge when useful. Repeat a precise
technical term when a synonym would imply a different concept. Use a transition
only when it expresses a relationship the material actually supports; do not add
an inferential transition merely to make the prose seem smoother.

**Participants and actions.** Make important participants and actions easy to
find. Prefer a direct verb when it preserves meaning. Use active or passive voice
according to topic and evidence; do not invent an actor to avoid the passive.

**Emphasis.** Put important information where the reader can notice and use it.
An explanation's main point often belongs early, but other genres may differ.
Sentence and clause endings can give important new information useful weight.
Treat this as a diagnostic, not a fixed slot: topic continuity, passive voice, or
a necessary qualification may deserve priority. Necessary qualifications are not
clutter.

**Syntax.** Avoid long interruptions before a sentence's main verb or completion.
Move qualifications only when they still modify the right claim. Split at real
conceptual boundaries. Use parallel grammar without smoothing meaningful
differences into false symmetry.

**Concision and rhythm.** Remove redundant framing, empty intensifiers, repeated
conclusions, and roundabout phrases when they add no distinction or useful
rhetorical function. Keep prerequisites, examples, definitions, qualifications,
uncertainty, and useful signposting. A longer explanation is correct when the
reader needs it.

There are no universal bans on hedging, transitions, passive voice,
nominalizations, abstract nouns, technical vocabulary, lists, long sentences, or
words associated with generated prose. Judge what each expression contributes.

Preserve deliberate humor, formality, restraint, pacing, and repetition when they
serve the author and task. An effective passage may need no change.

## Verify and deliver

Compare the result with the source or supplied facts. Check both directions: what
was lost, and what was added?

Verify that meaning, uncertainty, conditions, relationships, protected material,
and required content survive; no unsupported actor, premise, inference, hierarchy,
or certainty was added; and the revision stayed within scope. If completeness or
traceability matters, required material must remain present and sufficiently
discoverable.

Check that attention serves the requested communicative purpose without hiding
important qualifications; important organizing relationships are supported rather
than manufactured; any introduced or retained editable local abstraction earns
the work it asks of the reader; and established terminology and the author's
appropriate voice remain intact.

Then read continuously for coherence, emphasis, and avoidable reader effort.

Match the requested deliverable. For an edit, give the edited text. For critique,
identify the most consequential problems and show concrete alternatives. Explain
only important choices or unresolved ambiguities unless a teaching-oriented review
was requested. Do not append a ritual scorecard.

Editorial judgment is not fact-checking, and writing authority is not publication
authority. Domain evidence, repository rules, citation and authorship-disclosure
requirements, and tool permissions continue to govern.
